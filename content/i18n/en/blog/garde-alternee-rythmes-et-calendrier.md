**Shared custody**, called **alternating residence** (*résidence alternée*) in French law, means the child lives in turn with each parent. It can be agreed by both parents or set by the family judge (*juge aux affaires familiales*, article 373-2-9 of the French Civil Code). Once the principle is settled, a very practical question quickly follows: **which schedule should you choose, and how do you keep track week after week?**

## The most common schedules

### Alternate weeks

This is the most widespread schedule: the child spends a full week with one parent, then the next week with the other. The handover often takes place on Friday after school or on Monday morning.

- **Strengths**: few transitions, an easy pattern to remember, each parent has “full” weeks.
- **Points to watch**: seven days without seeing one parent can feel long for a young child.

### The 2-2-3 schedule

The child spends two days with one parent, two days with the other, then three days (the weekend) with the first. The following week, everything is reversed.

- **Strengths**: the child never goes more than three days without seeing each parent.
- **Points to watch**: many handovers, which require homes close to each other and good communication between parents.

### The 2-2-5-5 schedule

Each parent always has the same weekdays (for example Monday and Tuesday for one, Wednesday and Thursday for the other), and weekends alternate.

- **Strengths**: fixed days, easy to fit around activities and work schedules.
- **Points to watch**: like 2-2-3, it requires both homes to be close to the school.

## How to choose?

There is no ideal schedule that suits every family. A few criteria help you decide:

- **The child’s age**: shorter alternations are often recommended for very young children, who cope less well with long separations from either parent. A paediatrician, a psychologist or a family mediator can help you see clearly.
- **The distance between the two homes**: the further the homes are from school, the more tiring short schedules become.
- **The parents’ working hours**: shifts, irregular hours, travel.
- **The child’s views**: depending on their age and maturity, the child can ask to be heard by the judge (article 388-1 of the Civil Code).

A schedule is not set in stone. Many families start with short alternations, then move to alternate weeks as the child grows up.

## School holidays

Holidays often follow a different rule from the school year. The most common solution is to **split each holiday period in half**, alternating each year which parent has the first half (for example: even years for one, odd years for the other).

Two points to check:

- **Your education authority’s school calendar.** Overseas, the education authorities of French Guiana, Martinique, Guadeloupe, Réunion and Mayotte have their own calendars, different from mainland France. If one parent lives in another territory, specify which calendar applies.
- **53-week years.** With an “even weeks, odd weeks” rule, a 53-week year can create two weeks in a row with the same parent. It is best to state in your agreement how this case is handled, or to reason in dates rather than week numbers.

## Putting the agreement in writing

A verbal agreement works as long as everything is going well. In case of disagreement, a written agreement mainly protects the child by avoiding constant renegotiation. You can:

- draft a **parenting agreement** and have it **approved by the family judge**: it then has the force of a judgment;
- use **family mediation** to build this agreement together;
- if there is no agreement, **apply to the judge**, who will set the child’s residence and the schedule.

## Telling the Caf

With alternating residence, **family allowances can be shared** between both parents, at their joint request or in case of disagreement. Remember to declare your new situation too: the guide [declaring your separation to the Caf](/guide/guyane/declarer-separation-caf) lists the steps and documents.

## Keeping a common calendar

The biggest risk in shared custody is not the schedule itself but **misunderstandings**: who picks the child up on Friday, who has the Ascension long weekend, who takes them to the dentist. A shared calendar that both parents can check settles most of these questions before they turn into conflicts.

In Lien Parental, the **shared calendar** follows your custody schedule (alternate weeks, 2-2-3…) and shows where the child is each day. Appointments, activities and weekend swaps are dated and visible to both parents. To keep exchanges around this calendar calm, see also [communicating with the other parent](/blog/communiquer-avec-l-autre-parent).
