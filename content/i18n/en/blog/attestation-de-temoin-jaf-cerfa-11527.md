When the family judge (*juge aux affaires familiales*, JAF) has to decide on a child’s residence, contact arrangements or child support, they rely on the documents the parents provide. Among them, **witness statements** (*attestations de témoin*) allow someone close (grandparent, friend, neighbour, teacher, childminder…) to report what they **personally saw or heard**. A well-written statement can help the judge; a poorly written one may be set aside.

## What the law says

The form of a statement produced in court is set by **article 202 of the French Code of Civil Procedure**. The statement must:

- report **facts the author witnessed** or personally observed;
- give the author’s **surname, first names, date and place of birth, address and occupation**;
- state, where relevant, any **family relationship or relationship by marriage** with the parties, or any relationship of subordination, collaboration or interest;
- state that it is **made for use in court** and that the author knows that a **false statement exposes them to criminal penalties**;
- be **handwritten, dated and signed** by its author;
- come with a **copy of an official identity document** bearing the author’s signature (identity card, passport, residence permit…).

The official form **cerfa no. 11527*03** includes all these items. It is not compulsory, but it avoids forgetting one. It is available free of charge on [Service-Public.fr](https://www.service-public.gouv.fr/particuliers/vosdroits/R11307) (in French). The statement itself must be written in French.

## Who can testify?

Anyone who directly witnessed useful facts can write a statement: family, friends, neighbours, childcare professionals. There are a few limits:

- in **divorce proceedings**, the couple’s children cannot testify about the grievances raised by the spouses (article 259 of the Civil Code);
- a professional bound by **professional secrecy** (a doctor, for example) cannot disclose what they learned in the course of their work;
- a witness very close to one parent is not excluded, but the relationship must be stated: the judge will take it into account.

Avoid asking your child for a statement, even a teenager. If they wish to be heard, they can ask to be heard by the judge (article 388-1 of the Civil Code).

## Writing it well: facts, dates, places

The judge wants to understand the child’s actual daily life. A useful statement:

- **describes precise facts**: “On Saturday 14 September 2026, at around 10 am, I saw Ms X drop Lina off at the Kourou football club” rather than “she is a wonderful mother”;
- **dates and locates** each fact as far as possible;
- **separates what the witness saw** from what they were told: reported words (“he told me that…”) carry little weight;
- **stays measured**: value judgements, attacks on the other parent or generalities weaken the statement;
- **talks about the child**: schooling, health, activities, relationship with each parent.

> A good statement reads like a report, not a plea.

## Mistakes that get a statement set aside

- A statement **typed on a computer without a handwritten signature**, or without the wording on criminal penalties.
- **No copy of the witness’s identity document**.
- A text **dictated or written by the parent** and merely signed by the witness: judges often notice, and it undermines the credibility of the whole file.
- Statements that are **word-for-word identical**, signed by several people.

Important: making a **false statement** is an offence punishable by one year’s imprisonment and a €15,000 fine (article 441-7 of the French Criminal Code). Even in perfect form, a statement guarantees nothing: the judge **freely assesses** the weight of each document.

## How to hand it over

The witness gives the signed original, with the copy of their identity document, to the parent who asked for it. That parent passes it on to their lawyer, or adds it to their file if they appear before the judge without one.
